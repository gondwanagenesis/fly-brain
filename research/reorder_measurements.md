protocol: `flyloop.reorder.build_permutation`/`tile_liveness`, 1500 steps, dense

to narrow it down. These are exploratory and not part of the required regime table

|---:|---:|---:|

| 120 | 76.58% | 5.03% |
| 150 | 36.38% | 9.51% |

| 700 | 80.21% | 2.03% |
| 1000 | 84.50% | 0.42% |
| 10000 | 92.88% | 0.00% |

Two things fall out of this finer scan:

1. **The 10%-saved crossover sits at roughly live fraction ≈ 30-40%**, not at some
   particular stimulus count — e.g. n=150 (36% live) is right at the 10% line while
   n=120 (77% live, a *smaller* stimulus set) has already fallen to 5%.
2. **Live fraction is not monotonic in stimulus-neuron count.** Going from 100 to
   1000 driven neurons does not sweep live fraction smoothly through 30%→85% —
   individual draws land 36%-80% live depending on which specific neurons (and
   therefore which downstream fan-out hubs) get hit, because the recurrent
   connectome amplifies some draws far more than others. Whether tile-skipping is
   worth it is a property of **how much of the brain a given stimulus recruits**,
   not of how many sensory neurons are driven — a caller can't infer the payoff
   from stimulus size alone and should check the live fraction directly if it's
   close to the crossover.

## Verdict

**Does cell_type clustering generalise beyond the sugar protocol? Yes, directionally,
but the win is stimulus-size-dependent, not stimulus-identity-dependent, and it decays
fast.**

- `cell_type` is the best non-oracle key in *every* regime tested — sugar, P9
  walking, a single neuron, and all three broad-stimulation sizes. It is never beaten
  by `super_class+cell_class`, `morton`, or `hemibrain_type`. So the *ranking* of
  orderings is not sugar-specific: whatever makes `cell_type` group co-active
  neurons together (presumably that cells of the same type share inputs and
  therefore tend to fire together) holds for a walking command (P9), for a lone
  driven neuron, and for broad drive, not just for the headline sugar experiment.
  This answers the literal gating question: the optimisation is not "sugar-only."

- **But the *size* of the win is entirely a function of live fraction, and it
  collapses well before the brain is anywhere near saturated.** At tile=64:
  - sparse regimes (live ≤ ~8%: sugar, P9, single-neuron) — cell_type saves
    **60-98%** of tiles, close to the oracle ceiling (92-99.9%). This is the
    regime the whole optimisation was designed for, and it delivers.
  - the moment live fraction crosses roughly **30-40%**, cell_type's saved
    fraction falls under **10%** — a small win, but still a win, and still free
    (the permutation is precomputed and stimulus-independent).
  - by live fraction ~80-93% (broad 1000-10000), cell_type saves **0.4% down to
    0%** of tiles — indistinguishable from `none`. At that point almost every
    64-neuron tile contains at least one live neuron no matter how the index is
    permuted, because there just aren't enough dead neurons left to cluster away
    from live ones. **This is expected, not a bug**: tile-skipping is fundamentally
    a locality trick on top of sparsity, and once the network isn't sparse there is
    nothing left to exploit — grouping perfectly (the oracle) also degrades in the
    same regimes (oracle saved% falls from 92% at sugar to 7% at broad-10000),
    so the ceiling itself is collapsing, not just this particular key's ability
    to approach it.

- **Smaller tiles buy back some margin but don't change the qualitative picture.**
  Going from tile=64 to tile=16, cell_type's saved fraction on the sugar regime
  rises from 60.5% to 71.7% (finer granularity always helps, monotonically, for
  a fixed ordering — a smaller tile is strictly easier to keep entirely dead).
  But it doesn't rescue the broad regimes: at tile=16, broad-10000 still saves
  only 0.08% with cell_type (99.92% of even 16-wide tiles are live) and
  broad-1000 saves 3.51%. The live neurons in those regimes are too numerous and
  too structurally entangled (via cell_type-independent recurrent fan-out) to
  isolate into small dead pockets, no matter the tile width.

- **`super_class+cell_class` is a close, honest second** across every regime
  (e.g. sugar: 47.4% vs cell_type's 39.5% at tile=64) with near-total coverage
  (99.99%, vs cell_type's 98.9%) — a reasonable fallback if `cell_type` were
  ever unavailable or judged too fine-grained to maintain.

- **`morton` (pure spatial 3D locality) and `hemibrain_type` are meaningfully
  worse** than either annotation-based grouping at every regime, and
  `hemibrain_type` is close to `none` in the sparse regimes (89.7% vs 98.85% at
  sugar) simply because 76% of the brain has no hemibrain_type and collapses
  into one large group ordered by original (FlyWire) index — i.e. for most of
  the brain this key doesn't reorder anything. This is a coverage artifact, not
  evidence that hemibrain identity is a worse predictor of co-activity in
  principle; it is evidence that co-activity structure correlates with cell
  identity (`cell_type`, `super_class`/`cell_class`) much more strongly than
  with 3D physical proximity (`morton`) in this connectome, and that a key is
  only as useful as its coverage.

**Bottom line for the roadmap item this gates:** reordering by `cell_type` is
worth landing — it is a real, generalisable, essentially-free (one-time,
stimulus-independent) win of 60-98% tile reduction for the sparse/moderate
regimes the whole-brain kernel is normally run in (sugar, single-glomerulus,
walking-command stimuli), and it never makes anything *worse* than the current
order (`none` is always the worst key measured, oracle is always the best, and
cell_type sits well above `none` in every single row of every table above).
It should not be sold as a fix for broad/saturating stimulation — there,
neither this key nor any other reordering has meaningful headroom left, because
the live fraction itself, not the ordering, is the limiting factor.
